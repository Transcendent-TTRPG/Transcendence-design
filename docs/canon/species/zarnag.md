## 1. Identity

The Zarnag are feared because they live where rot, ruin, carrion, and taboo
meet. They are sought for the same reason.

Other peoples speak of them as corpse-eaters, plague walkers, grave defilers,
or night scavengers, but the Zarnag do not define themselves by desecration.
They define themselves by the labor no one else is willing to perform once
death stops behaving cleanly. In a world where battlefields do not empty
themselves, where pits of disease reopen after rain, where scavengers gather
around exposed dead, and where some remains seem to keep a dangerous pressure
even after life is gone, the Zarnag endure by going where disgust and fear
become survival work.

Their oldest civilizational claim is simple: the dead do not always leave
properly, and someone must deal with what remains.

They are not a people of abstract darkness. They are a people of bad remains,
foul thresholds, unfinished death, and necessary contact.

## 2. Body

Zarnag are medium humanoids descended from hyena stock, with sloped
shoulders, powerful necks, dense torsos, long forearms, heavy jaws, and a gait
that seems permanently ready to lower, lunge, drag, or circle. Their bodies
suggest endurance under bad conditions more than elegance or grace. They are
built for unstable ground, sudden violence, dragging burdens, tearing through
resistant matter, and lingering in places that would make others retch or
flee.

Their jaws are among their most defining features. A Zarnag bite is not
simply a natural weapon; it is a civilizational symbol of breach, contact, and
consequence. Their claws are shorter and more practical than theatrical:
useful for scrambling, digging, raking, gripping, and opening weak seams in
hide, cloth, wood, or flesh.

They possess strong tolerance for foul air, rot, blood mist, and the chemical
signatures of decay. This does not make them invulnerable to disease, but it
does mean they survive and interpret environments that would quickly sicken or
unsettle other peoples. Their laughter, too, is bodily before it is symbolic:
it is a stress signal, a pressure display, a way to harden group nerve in bad
places, and sometimes an intimidation instrument that puts others on edge even
before violence begins.

### Physical baseline

- **Height:** `1.61 - 1.73 m`
- **Weight:** `65 - 73 kg`
- **Lifespan:** `50 - 70 years`
- **Size category:** `Medium`

### Visible variation

Different Zarnag lineages and camps may emphasize heavier jaws, lighter frames,
rougher coats, stronger carrion tolerance, harsher laughter, or eyes better
adapted to low light and grave smoke. These differences matter socially and
ecologically, but do not yet imply rigid biological castes.

## 3. Culture

Zarnag culture formed around margins that other peoples avoid: battlefield
edges, plague trenches, carrion routes, refuse caverns, grave slopes, ruined
burial grounds, bone yards, and the half-abandoned outskirts of settlements too
frightened or too proud to manage their own dead properly.

Their central civilizational idea is the **dangerous remainder**. Corpses,
spoiled stores, stripped bones, broken graves, battlefield runoff, scavenger
trails, and contaminated pits are not passive aftermath to the Zarnag. They
are unstable residues that can poison the living, attract predators, spread
illness, distort mourning, and invite horrors best left unnamed. A settlement
that refuses this truth is not pure. It is simply delaying payment.

Because of this, Zarnag communities often specialize in tasks others consider
filthy, degrading, or spiritually dangerous:

- battlefield stripping and body recovery
- plague disposal and quarantine trench work
- grave correction and reburial
- carcass rendering and salvage
- scavenger-route management
- ruin clearance where the dead are not at rest

Respect inside Zarnag society does not come from innocence. It comes from
stamina, nerve, accuracy of judgment, and the ability to work near disgust
without losing clarity.

Laughter plays a large role in this culture. It releases fear, marks boldness,
pressures the weak, exposes unease, and helps groups keep rhythm in darkness or
low visibility. But the Zarnag are well aware that this same laughter can slide
into cruelty, domination, or delight in humiliation. Many of their moral
warnings revolve around the moment necessary hardness becomes appetite.

## 4. Theology and Cosmology

Zarnag cosmology begins from a brutal observation: death is not always a clean
departure. Something can linger in remains, in sites of mass death, in
unfinished rites, in flesh that rots wrong, or in pits where too many endings
were forced together under pressure.

Different Zarnag communities explain this differently. Some speak of corpse
hunger, some of grave echo, some of spiritual residue, some of the Limbo
pressing too close through unsealed death. But most agree on a common truth:
the interval between life and true departure can become dangerous if neglected.

This gives their death-work a sacred dimension. Burial, stripping, burning,
sorting, rendering, sealing, and moving remains are never merely practical.
They are acts of judgment against unfinished pressure.

Their horror is not rooted in “darkness” as a generic force. It is rooted in
the possibility that the world no longer finishes its dead properly. Battle
trenches, plague caverns, old sacrificial grounds, ruined cities, and exposed
ossuaries may all be signs of a wider cosmic failure. The Zarnag do not ask
whether death is frightening. They ask whether anything truly leaves at all.

This produces a theology that is suspicious, practical, and uncomfortably
close to decay. A people who make themselves necessary around corpses are always
at risk of being reshaped by them.

## 5. Organization and Internal Variation

Zarnag communities tend to organize around the labor they perform and the risks
they accept. They are not one uniform grave-culture.

Common community forms include:

- **Grave-edge camps:** small, mobile, practical communities managing burial
  lines, scavenger trails, and local contamination.
- **Battlefield followers:** larger or tougher groups attached to recurring war
  zones, army routes, or regions where dead accumulate faster than they can be
  honored.
- **Plague-front settlements:** quarantine-oriented communities built around
  harsh sanitation, corpse sorting, and infection control.
- **Ruin-strip circles:** bands or enclaves that work old ruins, tombs, and
  catastrophic sites where the dead are old, layered, or disturbed.
- **Bone-yard polities:** more established Zarnag centers built around pits,
  ossuaries, rendering grounds, or ancestral fields with long memory and strong
  internal taboos.

Important roles may include grave walkers, carrion judges, fear breakers,
trackers, trench wardens, renderers, and bone keepers. None of these are fully
fixed castes yet, but they point toward internal specialization.

Their major fault lines are moral as much as political:

- service to outsiders vs refusal of outsider contempt
- cleansing the dead vs exploiting the dead
- hardening the community vs normalizing cruelty
- preserving dangerous sites vs burning them empty
- practical survival vs sacral duty

The Zarnag know that one of their greatest dangers is internal: a community can
become monstrous without noticing the moment discipline turns into appetite.

## 6. Daily Life and Survival

Zarnag life is harsh, dirty, and tense even by the standards of Transcendence.
Their camps and settlements often exist near spoilage, open graves, scavenger
territory, unstable ruins, bad water, or the fringes of other peoples who want
their labor without wanting them close.

Food is practical before it is refined: dried meat, rendered fat, bitter roots,
broths, fungi, marrow, smoked offal, salvaged stores, and whatever can be
secured without crossing into corruption or disease. Knowing whether something
is merely foul or truly dangerous is a basic survival skill.

Children grow up learning things other peoples would rather never know:

- which flies mean fever and which mean ordinary rot
- which smells suggest opened graves
- what kind of silence means scavengers are near
- how quickly bad rain turns pits dangerous
- when laughter steadies a group and when it means someone is losing restraint

Initiation often revolves around judgment rather than bravery alone. It is not
enough to walk into a terrible place. One must return having interpreted it
correctly.

Daily fear is everywhere:

- a burial trench reopening after weather
- a scavenger swarm following the wrong scent toward camp
- a corpse field turning to sickness
- a ruin cache proving less dead than it looked
- a child misreading danger
- a neighbor settlement breaking contract and abandoning its dead nearby
- someone in the community beginning to enjoy the work too much

Old age is respected when it preserves judgment. Elder Zarnag are valued not
for softness, but for knowing what can be survived, what only looks survivable,
and which forms of contamination reach the spirit through habit rather than
wound.

## 7. External Relations

Few peoples encounter the Zarnag neutrally. Most fear them before understanding
them. Many also need them.

When plague pits open, when battlefields rot, when burial grounds fail, when
scavengers gather too close, when a ruin is thick with old remains, the Zarnag
become necessary. Yet this necessity rarely produces comfort or trust. They are
often hired reluctantly, paid badly, blamed quickly, and sent away once the
labor is done.

This gives them a recurring political position across the world: pushed to the
edge, summoned back when the edge spills inward.

Typical external pressures include:

- monstrous ecologies drawn to rot, fear, or exposed remains
- raids against camps burdened with corpse labor
- trade exclusion from settlements that still rely on Zarnag services
- theological hostility from peoples who read grave-work as defilement
- uneasy dependence from war bands, frontier towns, and ruin economies
- recurring suspicion that Zarnag benefit from the crises they manage

The Zarnag are therefore shaped by a contradiction: they are useful exactly
where others most want distance.

## 8. System Connections

### Competency affinities

Likely Zarnag strengths include:

- survival under contamination or scarcity
- corpse handling and practical anatomy
- intimidation and morale pressure
- tracking through foul or broken environments
- infection resistance and triage judgment

### Natural attack logic

Their current natural-weapon identity should center on:

- **Bite:** close breach, seizure, infection risk, and consequence after
  contact
- **Claws:** fast raking, chaining blows, scrambling, and tearing

### Arsenal fit

Likely weapon affinities:

- daggers and short blades
- axes, cleavers, and utilitarian cutters
- thrown tools
- natural bite and claws

Possible but less central:

- polearms for carcass distance or battlefield work
- shields for trench or plague-front duty
- heavy armor only in specialized roles

### Candidate species mechanics

Future Zarnag mechanics might explore:

- carrion tolerance
- twisted laughter as pressure signal
- corpse-field judgment
- contamination risk management

### Technique implications

Zarnag-origin Techniques should emerge from:

- grave labor
- survival around remains and contamination
- fear discipline
- scavenger-route reading
- infection threat after contact
- practical judgment under disgust

They should avoid collapsing into generic shadow magic or flavorless “dark
ritual” powers. Their horror is stronger when it comes through rot, remains,
threshold labor, and the unbearable fact that someone has to touch what others
abandon.

## 9. Design Notes

### What makes them distinct

The Zarnag should not be “dark hyena people.” Their core identity is that they
survive and build culture where death, refuse, contamination, and social taboo
become unavoidable labor.

### What to avoid

- direct copying of a real human culture
- generic cult-of-darkness aesthetics
- reducing them to villains
- making them simply plague-immune
- treating carrion and death only as style rather than survival logic

### Open questions

- What do the Zarnag believe happens when a corpse is neither properly consumed
  nor properly buried?
- Which Zarnag communities are more feared: battlefield followers, plague-edge
  camps, or ruin-strip circles?
- Do some Zarnag see themselves as protectors of the living, while others
  increasingly identify with the dead and the remainder?
- What kinds of ancient ruins or catastrophic zones first taught them that bad
  death can spread?
