# Module Design Axioms

**Authority:** These axioms are the design filter for all campaign module work.  
**Applies to:** Every module, every scene, every encounter, every NPC, every location.  
**Related:** `docs/campaign-module-reference.md`, `skills/module-development/SKILL.md`

These are not preferences — they are structural constraints. Any design decision that violates them should be revised, not justified.

---

## Axiom 1 — Organic system presence, not obsessive insertion

The systems exist in the world. The world does not exist to showcase the systems.

Vestigios, vínculos, ciclos autónomos, anomalías, entidades del Limbo, taumatic environments — these are features of the world that appear when the fiction produces them. They are not a checklist. A scene without taumatic elements is not an incomplete scene. An encounter without a ciclo autónomo is not a missed opportunity.

**What this means in practice:**
- Do not insert a system element because the module "hasn't shown it yet"
- Do not design a location around a mechanic — design it as a place, then ask what systems naturally live there
- The presence of a complex system should always be justifiable by the fiction, not by the production schedule

**The test:** If removing the system element would make the scene feel empty or missing something, the element was designed correctly. If removing it would not change the scene's identity, it was inserted.

## Axiom 2 — Arrhythmic cadence

This is the most important structural principle. The module must be unpredictable.

Not everything is brutal. Not everything is tense. Not everything is dark or horrifying or physically dangerous. Calm scenes exist and carry weight. Moments of mundane normalcy can be more unsettling than explicit horror, precisely because they exist in this world.

The module must actively resist formula:
- No fixed rotation of combat → rest → travel → combat
- Tension does not always resolve
- Resolution does not always follow tension
- The players should not be able to predict what kind of scene comes next from the rhythm of the previous one

**What this means in practice:**
- Scenes of genuine stillness are legitimate module content — not padding
- A scene can end without a combat, a discovery, or a reveal
- A scene that appears calm can end with something that was never telegraphed
- Horror lands harder when preceded by something that felt safe

**The test:** If a player who has read half the module can correctly predict the register of the next scene — danger, relief, mystery, social — the cadence is too rhythmic.

## Axiom 3 — Horror and hostility are texture, not constant tone

The setting is post-apocalyptic, cosmically hostile, and structurally brutal. That is true. It is not the pitch for every scene.

If every scene communicates "this world is dark and dangerous," the signal becomes noise. The horror of the setting is felt by contrast — by moments where something almost feels normal, and then doesn't. By what is absent. By what was once here and isn't.

**What this means in practice:**
- Do not end every location description with a reminder that the world is dangerous
- Do not add gore, dread, or visceral imagery to scenes that don't earn it
- The post-apocalyptic setting is already established — it does not need to be re-established in every paragraph
- A creature does not need to be described as terrifying to be terrifying

**The test:** If the module's tone would read the same whether it was set in a hostile zone or a relatively stable settlement, the texture is not doing its work.

## Axiom 4 — Progressive system exposure, exponential curve

This module introduces the system to players who do not know it. The exposure must follow a curve — not a front-loaded dump.

**The curve:**

| Phase | Systems present |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | Core rolls, ATB basics, base combat, competencies, one or two species traits |
| Early-mid | Techniques begin appearing, environmental conditions as pressure, species friction |
| Mid | Tactical depth increases, ciclos autónomos visible (biological), more technique variety |
| Late | Full tactical complexity, possible environmental taumatic elements, cosmic elements if earned |
| End | The system's full depth is available, but players have built up to it |

**What this means in practice:**
- Do not introduce vestigios and taumatic anomalies in the first session
- Do not front-load tactical complexity — let players establish rhythm before layering
- Each system introduction should feel like the world revealing itself, not like a tutorial popup
- A system that appears once and never again is not an introduction — it is noise

**The test:** A player new to the system should, by the end of the module, understand how all major systems work — not because they were explained, but because they lived through them in sequence.

## Axiom 5 — Setting cohesion over mechanical completeness

A place that feels real matters more than a place that showcases every mechanic.

Any location in the module should be able to answer:
- What is this place and why does it exist?
- Who was here before? What did they leave?
- What does this place want from the people in it? (Even if the answer is "nothing — it's indifferent")
- What would happen here if the players were never involved?

A mechanically rich location that does not answer these questions will feel like a game board. A mechanically sparse location that answers them will feel like a world.

**What this means in practice:**
- Design locations as places first, encounter spaces second
- History, atmosphere, and internal logic are not optional details — they are what makes the location a location
- Mechanical features (difficult terrain, environmental conditions, cover) should emerge from what the place is, not be bolted on for tactical variety

**The test:** Could a non-player read the location description and understand it as a place that exists? If the answer requires knowing the game's mechanical vocabulary, revise.

## Axiom 6 — Anti-cliché structure

The module's structural beats must not be predictable by genre convention.

**Banned openings:**
- Starting in a tavern or equivalent social gathering space
- A mysterious stranger approaching with a job
- "You all meet for the first time and need a reason to work together"
- A dramatic crisis that forces the players into immediate danger before they understand the world

**The entry principle:** The players enter a world that was already in motion. They are not the reason the world started moving. The module's opening should feel like arriving somewhere mid-situation — not like a game starting.

**What this means in practice:**
- The players' first scene should establish the world's texture before it establishes the plot
- The first threat they encounter should feel like a feature of this world, not like a narrative trigger
- Their reason for being together should be fictional and pre-established, not improvised at the table because "the module starts now"

**The test:** If the opening scene could be lifted and placed into a D&D module without modification, rewrite it.

---

## Axiom 7 — Long arc, not exhaustive

This module covers a portion of the world — not all of it. It has an internal arc that functions as a unit.

Multiple regions and species appear. Not all regions. Not all species. The players will finish this module understanding the world as a place with depth they have not yet reached — not as a place they have seen in full.

**What this means in practice:**
- Unresolved threads are correct — they are evidence that the world continues beyond the module
- Species that appear but are not fully explored are intentional — players should want to learn more
- The module's ending closes the immediate arc without closing the world
- Not every question the module raises needs an answer within the module

**The test:** At the end of the module, do players feel they have seen everything, or do they feel they have seen enough to want more? The second answer is correct.

---

## Using These Axioms

When designing any element of a module — a scene, a location, an NPC, an encounter, a piece of loot, a piece of lore — run it through this filter:

1. Is this system element present because the fiction produced it, or because I wanted to show the system? (Axiom 1)
2. Does this scene's register match what the surrounding scenes need, or am I following a pattern? (Axiom 2)
3. Is the horror/danger content earning its presence, or am I reinforcing tone that is already established? (Axiom 3)
4. Is the system complexity appropriate for where we are in the arc? (Axiom 4)
5. Does this location exist as a place, or as a game board? (Axiom 5)
6. Is this structural beat predictable by genre convention? (Axiom 6)
7. Does this element serve the arc, or am I trying to fit too much world into it? (Axiom 7)

If any answer is "wrong," revise the element before moving forward.
